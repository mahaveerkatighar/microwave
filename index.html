How far can Wi-Fi reach?

Wi-Fi is an invisible radio wave, and the farther it travels the weaker it gets. This project measures that fading with two small ESP32 boards and shows the results live.

How it works ▶ Try the demo Live data Graphs

How it works

📡

1. Send

One board (the transmitter) broadcasts a Wi-Fi signal at 2.4 GHz, a bit like a tiny radio station.

📶

2. Measure

A second board (the receiver) listens and measures how strong that signal is at a known distance.

🔘

3. Save

Pressing a button averages the latest readings and saves one measurement, together with the distance.

☁️

4. Show

The measurement goes to the cloud (Firebase) and appears on this page within seconds.

💡 Key idea: in open space, doubling the distance makes the signal about 4× weaker (6 dB less). Walls, people and reflections make it weaker and less predictable. We measure the real thing and compare it with the theory.

Words explained

RSSI

Received Signal Strength Indicator: a number the Wi-Fi chip reports to say how strong the signal it received was. It is an indicator, not a laboratory-grade power measurement.

dBm

A way of writing power on a log scale. 0 dBm is one milliwatt. Wi-Fi signals at the receiver are tiny, so the numbers are negative: −40 is strong, −80 is weak.

Path loss

How much a signal weakens between transmitter and receiver, in decibels (dB). Free-space path loss is the theoretical loss over empty space.

Line of sight

Nothing between the two boards. A wall or a person in between usually weakens the signal further.

Multipath and fading

Indoors the signal bounces off walls, floors and furniture and arrives by several routes that can add up or cancel out. This makes readings swing by several dB even when nothing moves.

2.4 GHz

A microwave frequency band used by Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. This is why the project belongs to Microwave Engineering.

RSSI is an uncalibrated indicator reported by the ESP32 Wi-Fi subsystem. This is a practical propagation study, not a calibrated RF power measurement.
